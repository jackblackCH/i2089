## Analyze (descriptive)

*What happens when an engineering lead reads this page as it is.* 5 layer-cascade consequences, 2 integrative compounds.

### 1. Ten chunks for one decision [Cognitive Load]
*The page asks the reader to hold more parts than a mind holds at once.*

Working memory holds about 3–5 chunks. The page presents 10 blocks, and entry 02 alone carries 50 words with three different ideas (setup, teaching, goal). The reader must keep "what it is" in mind until "how we work" (entry 04) to understand what they buy. Novice readers (a CTO who has not used agents) pay the most: every term is new. Expert readers skim the labels, but the labels do not form one story (a definition, a teaching list, a CV, a format, a safety rule). On a phone the 10 blocks become 1,230px of scroll.

**What happens:** readers skim the labels, do not reach "How we work", and leave with "AI coaching, something" instead of a plan. Over weeks this shows as short visits and few mails.

**Citations:** Cowan (2010) for the 3–5 chunk limit; Sweller (1988) for extraneous load from split information.

### 2. The first screen shows a definition, not the offer [First Impression]
*The first thing under the title explains AI agents, not what Marc does for the team.*

The title "Agentic Engineering for teams" is clear. The first entry then defines a tool category, which every lead in 2026 already knows. The offer (workshop, coaching) sits in entry 04, below the fold on most screens. The contact moved to the foot, so on 1440×900 the first screen holds no action at all.

**What happens:** in the first seconds the page reads as an explainer, not as a service. Leads who arrive from home or search ("agentic engineering coaching Zürich") do not see the format or the next step without scrolling.

**Citations:** Lindgaard et al. (2006) for first-impression gating; Fogg (2003) for prominence before interpretation.

### 3. Lines that read as orders, and four names for one thing [Processing Fluency]
*Some sentences sound like commands to the reader, and the tool changes its name four times.*

Entry 02 starts "Set up Claude Code in your existing projects … Show your team how to prompt". With the "I" dropped, a present-tense verb reads as an imperative: the reader is told to set up and to show. In the same sentence "your team" points at the reader, which makes the misreading stronger. Entry 03 has no verb at all ("Claude Code, Codex and the modern LLM tools every day for the last year"). The tool is called "AI coding agents", "Claude Code", "LLMs", "the modern LLM tools" and "the AI". The labels mix types: a question ("What it is"), a first-person phrase ("What I teach"), nouns ("Experience", "Review and tests"), "we" ("How we work"). Each break costs a re-read, and felt effort lowers felt truth. The visual system (type, spacing, colour) is consistent; the friction is verbal.

**What happens:** readers re-read entries 02 and 03, and the effort transfers to the offer ("sounds complicated"). Non-native readers (much of the Swiss market) feel it most.

**Citations:** Reber & Schwarz (1999) for fluency as felt truth; Alter & Oppenheimer (2009) for fluency effects below awareness.

### 4. Proof that does not match the claim [Perception Bias]
*The page claims faster delivery, but the proof shown is frontend work.*

"Delivers faster" is a claim with no number. The proof at the foot ("Selected engineering work": AXA, air up®, Talentir, Branded Goods) is frontend work; the label is honest, but System 1 notices that no proof is about AI. The strongest trust signal for a cautious lead is the safety rule (entry 05: review and tests), and it sits last, after the reader has formed a risk prior. Loss aversion makes "will this break our code?" weigh about twice "will this make us faster?".

**What happens:** skeptical leads (corporates) discount the page; enthusiastic leads (startups) are not given a concrete reason to pick Marc over a course.

**Citations:** Kahneman & Tversky (1979) for loss aversion; Seckler et al. (2015) for trust as a perceptual output.

### 5. No visible next step [Decision Architecture]
*The page ends on a mail button that most readers do not see without scrolling.*

The format (workshop day → coaching → optional embed) is the decision path, but it is entry 04 of 5 and is not linked to the action. The contact is below the first screen at 1440×900 and far below on phones. The step labels do not say what the lead gets after each step, so the commitment distance is unclear (a day? weeks?). The step lengths are still open (TODO).

**What happens:** readers who are convinced must scroll to find the action; readers who are unsure have no small first step ("a workshop day") framed as the action.

**Citations:** Pirolli & Card (1999) for information scent; Trope & Liberman (2010) for concrete language near the action.

### Integrative compounds

#### A. Effort reads as risk [Cross-layer: L0 × L2 × L3]
*When the page is hard to read, the service feels risky.*

The topic already carries risk (AI writing production code). High load (1) and verbal friction (3) are felt as effort, and effort is attributed to the offer, not to the page. The safety rule that would lower the risk sits last (4).

**What happens:** the reading problem and the trust problem are one problem: fixing the copy raises trust more than adding proof.

**Citations:** Alter & Oppenheimer (2009) for fluency-to-judgment transfer; Kahneman (2011) for attribution of felt ease.

#### B. The style rule leaks across pages [Cross-layer: L2 × ecosystem]
*The same "drop the I" rule makes offer lines read as orders on /frontend too.*

The implied-first-person rule works for past work ("Led…", "Built…"). In present tense it turns into an imperative ("Design and build interfaces…", "Set up Claude Code…"). The rule lives in the voice skill, so every new page inherits the misreading.

**What happens:** the fix belongs in the style rule, not only on /ai.

**Citations:** Applied prediction; no direct framework citation. Closest adjacent: Reber & Schwarz (1999) for fluency.

## Solve (prescriptive)

**Problem:** an engineering lead wants to know, in under a minute, what changes for the team, how it runs, why Marc, and how to start. The page makes them read a definition, a mixed list and a CV in that order, with friction in the sentences and the action out of view.

| Layer | Constraint | Violation | Requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| L0 | 3–5 chunks | 10 blocks, 50-word entry | **R1:** at most 3 content entries; at most 2 sentences and about 30 words each. |
| L1 | first screen gates the rest | definition first, no action in the first screen | **R2:** the first entry states the outcome for the team; title, outcome and the action fit the first screen at 1440×900. |
| L2 | felt fluency | imperative misreading, verbless lines, 4 names for the tool, mixed label grammar | **R3:** every sentence has a subject and a verb; offers in present tense keep "I"; one name for the tool ("AI coding agents", Claude Code and Codex as examples); labels in one grammar (nouns). |
| L3 | trust is perceptual; losses weigh 2× | safety last, unproven "faster" | **R4:** the safety rule (review and tests, engineers decide) sits in the first entry; no unproven claim ("faster" only as a goal, or with a real number later). |
| L4 | scent, concrete language near the action | format buried, action out of view | **R5:** the format is the path: three concrete steps, the first step small (a workshop day), followed by the action. |

**Citations:** Cowan (2010) for R1; Lindgaard et al. (2006) for R2; Reber & Schwarz (1999) for R3; Kahneman & Tversky (1979) for R4; Pirolli & Card (1999) for R5.

**Solution (satisfies R1–R5):** three entries, 110 words instead of 201.

- **01 The result:** Your team works with AI coding agents like Claude Code and Codex in the projects it already has. Your engineers decide, and every change still goes through your code review and tests.
- **02 How it works:**
  1. A workshop day: I set up Claude Code in your codebase, with rules and skills that fit it.
  2. Coaching on your real tickets, in 1:1s and pair programming: which prompts and workflows fit which task.
  3. Optional: I join your team as engineer and coach.
- **03 Who coaches:** I have engineered software for sixteen years, usually as a lead, and coaching has been part of it. I have used Claude Code and Codex every day for the last year.

Then the selected work and the contact, as now. With 110 words the content band is shorter, so at 1440×900 the contact returns into the first screen (R2, R5).

**Test of the current page against R1–R5:** fails R1 (10 blocks), R2 (definition first, action below the fold), R3 (imperative 02, verbless 03, 4 names, mixed labels), R4 (safety last); partly meets R5 (steps exist, not tied to the action).

**Gap:** the cross-page rule (B). Present-tense offer lines need "I" back, on /frontend too, and the voice skill should say so.
