## The Ferrari problem

It's late, the coffee is cold, and I type one sentence into a chat window. Ninety seconds later, a landing page is on my screen: gradients, and a hero that glows. Five years ago, that would have been a week of work.

It's a strange feeling, a little like magic and a little like cheating.

Then I open another tab and see the same page. Different product, same gradient, same glow. Someone else had typed a sentence much like mine.

That's the catch. When anyone can build a Ferrari, nobody turns to watch one go by. The bar doesn't drop. It rises, to a place no single prompt can reach.

### Where taste lives

That place is taste. It means knowing what to leave out, and knowing when a thing is done.

Taste lives in a designer's head, built from years of looking and failing. The machine has none of that. It has seen almost everything, and by default it hands you the average. Smooth, and easy to forget.

So the job changes. Making things has become cheap. Moving the picture from my head into the machine has not.

## A small experiment

Try this yourself. Give a model the sentence most of us start with:

```text
Design a landing page for a Swiss law firm.
```

You'll most likely get the average: a dark hero, a gradient, three feature cards and a big button. There's nothing wrong with it, and nothing you'll remember either.

### The picture instead of the product

Now describe the picture, not the product:

```text
Design a landing page for a Swiss law firm.
It should feel like a well-kept archive: paper white, black ink,
one serif for headings, generous margins, no gradients, no shadows.
The only colour is a deep red, used once, for the call to action.
Nothing moves unless the visitor asks for it.
```

Same model, same minute. But now it has something to aim for. The words carry the taste.

### Taste as a skill

The next step is to stop retyping that paragraph. I put it in a skill: a small file the model reads before it starts.

```markdown
# Taste: quiet editorial
- One serif for headings, one sans for text. Never a third.
- Colour is a signal, not a mood. One accent, used once per page.
- If an element doesn't help the reader decide, it goes.
- Motion only in answer to the user, never on its own.
```

Now the taste travels: to the next project, and to the next person on the team.

## Skill engineering

Here is what I've come to believe. The machine doesn't read English the way we do. It compiles our words into choices, and a sentence that is perfectly clear to me can compile into something I never meant.

### Who wins the trophy

Learning that compiler is a new craft. It sits somewhere between design and engineering, and it doesn't have a proper name yet. I call it skill engineering.

The trophy doesn't go to the fastest prompt. It goes to whoever finds the words for the picture in their head.
